Payment systems are often described as neutral plumbing. They are not. A rail decides who may participate, which risks are mutualized, how quickly claims become final, and what kinds of institutions can sit at the edge of the network.

> The interesting question is rarely whether a payment is digital. It is who maintains the ledger, who guarantees finality, and who can alter the rules.

## Three questions I keep returning to

1. **What is the settlement asset?** A bank deposit, central-bank money, or a privately issued token implies a different allocation of risk.
2. **Where does finality live?** Fast messages do not necessarily mean fast settlement.
3. **Who can build at the edge?** Open access can produce useful competition, but only if the core remains credible.

## On the reading desk

- Work from the Bank for International Settlements on unified ledgers and tokenization.
- Historical accounts of clearing houses as private coordination mechanisms.
- Canadian policy work on payment modernization and monetary sovereignty.

These notes are provisional. I am using them to connect technical architecture to institutional design—the two are usually discussed separately, even when each constrains the other.[^1]

[^1]: This is also why I find payments a useful subject: implementation details quickly become questions about governance.
